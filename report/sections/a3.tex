\subsection{Leading research groups}\label{sec:groups}

Erik Cambria's research group at Nanyang Technological University
studies how commonsense knowledge can help a system judge the
sentiment of a text and explain its decision. SenticNet 7 and
SenticNet 8 show how this line of work developed. The first paper
focuses on building a knowledge base that links different expressions
to shared concepts. The second extends this approach by examining
what those concepts mean in a sentence.

\paragraph{SenticNet 7 (2022).}
Cambria et al.\citep{cambria2022senticnet} use XLNet to propose
words and phrases that could express a similar meaning in context.
A similarity measure based on links to emotion concepts in ConceptNet
helps separate expressions that may look alike in context but carry
different feelings. The resulting knowledge base includes both
single words and phrases. For example, it treats ``dead right'' as
a phrase with its own meaning, so the word ``dead'' does not determine
its sentiment on its own.

The authors compared SenticNet 7 with 20 sentiment lexicons on ten
datasets, including Amazon reviews and IMDb reviews. They attributed
part of its performance to wider vocabulary coverage, since missing
entries were a common source of errors for other lexicons. Sarcasm
and sentences that express opposing opinions about different targets
still caused errors. A larger vocabulary helps the system recognize
more expressions, while deciding which opinion applies to which
target remains a separate problem.

\paragraph{SenticNet 8 (2024).}
SenticNet 8 combines a commonsense knowledge base with a hierarchical
attention network that identifies which tokens are most relevant
to the sentiment decision\citep{cambria2024senticnet}. Its text
processing goes from standardizing the wording to grouping expressions
with similar meanings, then to describing the situation those
expressions refer to. The paper illustrates this with several ways
of describing a phone purchase. Phrases such as ``buy a phone'' and
``pay for a phone'' can both be represented as a change in who owns
the phone. This example makes the purpose of the intermediate
representation easier to see.

On the Sentiment140 polarity task, SenticNet 8 achieved 88.80\%
accuracy, compared with 85.07\% for the RoBERTa baseline in the same
paper. The test texts were tweets, so applying the method to product
reviews would require a separate evaluation.

\paragraph{Relevance to product reviews.}
Review 1123 in Section~\ref{sec:analysis} illustrates the issue of opinion targets.
The reviewer describes old plastic luggage tags cracking and
breaking, then explains why the new leather tag was a better
purchase. The initial RoBERTa ensemble predicted a negative label,
whereas the final human label was positive. An analysis that links the damage to the
old tags and the recommendation to the new tag could preserve the
distinction that matters for this review. Whether SenticNet would
classify this example correctly remains an open question for a
direct comparison on the same data.
